## Slide 5 — What AI is at work

At work, AI is not one thing. It can summarise, draft, translate, analyse, automate, help us think through options.

The mental model that helps isn't *magic brain*. It's **a very fast assistant that needs clear instructions and human review.**

That single shift in framing fixes most of the bad AI experiences I see in our business.

## Slide 6 — Fluent ≠ Accurate

AI can be confidently wrong. It can produce a great-looking answer with weak facts behind it. Outdated information. Misunderstood context. It can even invent things if you ask in a vague way.

So the goal is not blind trust. The goal is **better human-AI teamwork.**

## Slide 7 — Six Golden Rules

If you remember nothing else from this session, remember these six lines.

1. **Protect data.** No confidential content in public AI tools.
2. **Give context.** Situation, audience, objective, constraints.
3. **Ask for format.** Table, email, checklist, summary, outline.
4. **Verify facts.** Especially numbers, names, dates, policies.
5. **Iterate.** Improve, shorten, expand, challenge.
6. **You stay accountable.** AI helps. You sign off.

Rule six is the most important. AI can help write your email. It should not become the person responsible for your email.

## Slide 8 — CRAFT-V

A good prompt has up to six parts. We call it **CRAFT-V**.

- **C — Context:** What's the situation?
- **R — Role:** Who should AI act as?
- **A — Action:** What exactly should it do?
- **F — Format:** What should the output look like?
- **T — Tone:** How should it sound?
- **V — Verify:** What should it flag, list as assumptions, or call out as needing your review?

You don't write all six every time. The point is just enough direction for the AI to be useful — and a built-in habit of verification, because what comes back is your responsibility.

## Slide 9 — Prompt Battle (Mentimeter)

Compare these two prompts.

**Prompt A** — "Act as a business analyst. Review this monthly sales report and identify the key trends and risks. Output as a short summary."

**Prompt B** — "Act as a business analyst. Review this monthly sales report for a regional MD. Identify the key trends, anomalies, and risks — and the three actions the MD should take this week. Output as a table: Observation, Evidence, Implication, Recommended action, Confidence. List any assumptions."

Both are plausible. Vote which one will produce a more useful answer.

[Wait for vote. Reveal result.]

The difference: A asks for *observations*. B asks for *decisions*. For a regional MD on a Monday morning, decisions are what matter. Same AI. Very different output.

## Slide 15 — Workflow patterns

I don't want to position this as one specific project. The bigger point is that many work processes share the same skeleton.

Think about weekly reports, eCAPEX-style approvals, visitor registration, issue logs, training requests, or any process where people chase updates manually. The pattern is usually:

- **Capture:** use Forms, SharePoint lists, Excel Online or Power Apps to collect the input once.
- **Route:** use Power Automate to send approvals, reminders, ownership changes and escalations.
- **Track:** create visibility: status, ageing, bottlenecks, pending actions and audit trail.
- **Assist:** use Copilot to draft summaries, explain trends and prepare follow-up — but still with human review.

The lesson: AI value compounds when we connect **capture → route → track → assist**, instead of treating every request as a separate manual task.

## Slide 17 — Code and data assistance

For our engineers, MES users, and data folks: AI is a pair-coding partner.

- Office Scripts (TypeScript/JavaScript for Excel automation)
- Power Query M — transformation errors explained in plain English
- DAX — "why is this measure returning blank?"
- Regex — the one part of programming nobody enjoys
- Error messages — what they mean in your context

Worked example:

> "Write an Office Script that loops through all worksheets, finds tables matching a naming pattern, and returns the row count of each as a Markdown table."
>
> Then: "Refactor so it handles sheets with no matching table and logs which ones were skipped. Add JSDoc comments."

Notice: no company data needed in the prompt. Copilot helps with the *shape* of the code; the actual data stays in your workbook.

## Slide 18 — Approved-for-work vs personal-learning

This is the most important governance slide. Two columns, one rule.

**Approved for Filtrona work**

- Copilot Chat with Enterprise Data Protection
- Copilot in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, Teams (where licensed)
- Clipchamp, Power Automate, Power Apps, Power BI
- Copilot Studio agents on our tenant

**Fine for personal learning only**

- ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini (consumer versions)
- OpenAI and other public image or video generators
- InVideo, public coding assistants
- Anything where the prompt may be used to train a model

The critical principle, on the banner at the bottom of the slide: **confidentiality lives in the content, not the filename.** Removing "Filtrona" from a file does not make the data safe to paste into a public tool.

## Slide 19 — Quiz: data classification

Mentimeter question:

> "Can I upload a customer pricing workbook to a free public AI website if I remove the filename and the customer's name?"

Options: Yes / Depends / No.

[Wait for vote.]

Correct answer: **No**.

The principle is data classification. Classification follows *content*, not metadata. Pricing terms, margin structure, customer identity — these are sensitive whether the file is called `pricing-Q3.xlsx` or `untitled.xlsx`.

The practical test: **if a competitor or this customer saw the content, would there be a problem?** If yes, it doesn't go in a public tool, no matter how you rename it.
